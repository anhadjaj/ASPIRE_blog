The idea

We've spent years making computers smaller. What if we could make them disappear?

Every time we want help from an AI today, we perform the same little ritual: reach for a phone, unlock it, find the right app, type something, attach something, wait, read, and then put the phone away.

It works. But it is still a conversation with a screen.

What if the computer already knew where you were looking? What if you could just ask a question while walking, working, travelling, building something, or standing in front of something you didn't understand?

That question became ASPIRE.

Just ask. hands-free

There was no single “smart glasses” platform waiting to be assembled. The camera, microphone, audio, wake detection, connectivity, AI orchestration, tool execution and the interaction model all had to be built from the ground up and made to work together.

The experience

It should feel like talking to someone, not operating a machine.

Just talk.

You shouldn't have to learn a command language to use your own assistant. ASPIRE is built around natural conversation: ask something, describe something, or tell VIPER what you want to happen.

Say the wake word, speak the way you would to a friend, and let go. The glasses work out when you've finished.

Wake → listen → respondA deliberate interaction loop instead of an always-on cloud microphone.
Voice-first outputReplies are written to be heard, not read: short, spoken and natural.

Your point of view becomes context.

The camera changes the relationship between you and the AI. Instead of describing a thing and hoping the model understands, you let it look at the thing with you.

Read a sign. Understand a screen. Ask about an object. Translate something in front of you. The image isn't an attachment. It's part of the conversation.

First-person visionThe camera sees from the same perspective as the wearer.
Visual translationAsk VIPER to read or translate any text you can see.
Focused answersThe reply is about the thing that matters, not a description of everything in frame.

The interesting part isn't seeing. It's knowing why you're showing it.

A camera can capture a picture. An assistant needs to understand the question around that picture.

VIPER combines your spoken intent with what the camera sees, so the same sensor supports completely different requests. “What is this?” is different from “Translate this,” which is different from “Does this code look right?”

Multimodal interactionVoice and vision work together as one feature, not two.
Context-aware reasoningThe model answers your actual question rather than narrating the scene.

And sometimes, the right answer isn't an answer.

VIPER turns intention into action. Ask for a photo and it captures one. Ask about your inbox and it reads your inbox, instead of pretending it already knows what's in it.

That is the step from chatbot to assistant: understanding what should happen next.

What changed between V1 and V2.

01

Connections kept warm

V1 paid the cost of opening a fresh connection on every request. V2 reuses live connections and warms them up ahead of time, so the first byte doesn't wait on a handshake.

02

Speak first, log later

Saving photos and conversation history used to sit in the critical path. Now ASPIRE answers you first and does the bookkeeping afterwards.

03

A shorter wait at the end

The end-of-speech window is tighter, so the glasses stop listening sooner once you've actually finished talking.

04

Right-sized frames

Smaller camera frames mean less to capture, move and process, without losing what the model needs to answer well.

05

Talk while thinking

Replies are turned into speech sentence by sentence, so you hear the first words while the rest is still being prepared.

The intelligence

ASPIRE is the body. VIPER is the mind.

THE BODY

ASPIRE

The physical system: the frame, camera, microphone, audio hardware and embedded controller that sit with you in the real world and turn light and sound into signals.

THE MIND

VIPER

The intelligence layer. Its job isn't just to generate text. It decides what kind of response you need, whether visual context matters, whether a tool should be called, and when an action should replace another sentence.

All the complexity should disappear behind one very simple interface: talk to it.

For the curious

The engineering underneath.

If the first half is the story of what ASPIRE feels like, this is the part for people who want to know how I actually made it work.

A tiny computer with very non-tiny ambitions.

The prototype uses a Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense as the embedded core. It provides the camera and microphone interface while staying compact enough to wear.

The challenge isn't getting each component to work. It's making them work together without turning the glasses into a hot, slow development board strapped to your face.

CONTROLLER

Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense with embedded PSRAM.

CAMERA

OV2640 camera used for first-person visual input.

AUDIO

Digital microphone on an I2S path, with a dedicated amplifier for output.

Listening only when listening matters.

Continuous audio streaming is expensive. The firmware uses local voice-activity detection based on RMS energy to know when speech is actually happening.

Audio is processed in blocks, with a loudness threshold and trailing silence deciding where a turn ends, and a hard cap on recording time. “Always listening” becomes a controlled interaction loop.

LOCAL VAD

RMS-based speech detection runs on the device, before anything is transmitted.

TURN CONTROL

Speech debounce and trailing silence prevent accidental short triggers.

FAIL-SAFE

A maximum recording duration prevents uncontrolled buffer growth.

The camera is not an accessory. It's a sensor for context.

Each OV2640 frame is captured into available memory and passed through the vision pipeline. Because of how the sensor is mounted, the system explicitly accounts for its orientation when interpreting images.

Resolution and image handling are deliberately constrained around the latency and memory budget of a wearable, rather than chasing maximum image quality.

CAPTURE

Frames are handled directly by the embedded system.

ORIENTATION

Sensor configuration and model-level instructions compensate for the mounting geometry.

VISION RULES

VIPER is instructed not to invent visual details and to focus on the requested subject.

From “answer me” to “do this.”

The assistant is given a set of executable tools alongside your request. If the model decides a tool is appropriate, it returns a structured action which the system executes.

That's what lets one voice interface move between conversation and action: retrieving information, using connected services, or triggering the camera.

TOOL CALLING

Structured model outputs map spoken intent to executable functions.

CONNECTED SERVICES

Integrations include email, calendar, maps, music, search and cloud storage.

LOCAL ACTIONS

Hardware operations such as photo capture can be short-circuited locally, with no round trip.

The hard part was deciding what the glasses should not do.

An ESP32-S3 is powerful for its size, but it isn't a miniature data centre. Instead of forcing every AI workload onto the frame, ASPIRE treats the glasses as a fast sensory endpoint and sends heavier reasoning to external compute.

That keeps the wearable focused on what it's uniquely good at: sensing the world, capturing your intent and responding quickly. It also lets the intelligence side improve independently of the hardware.

EDGE

Wake detection, audio capture, camera control and hardware actions.

REASONING

Large-model inference handles language, multimodal understanding and tool selection.

ORCHESTRATION

Keeping sensing and intelligence separate leaves room for a more modular, MCP-based architecture.
