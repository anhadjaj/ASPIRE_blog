01 / THE IDEA

We've spent years making computers smaller. I wanted to make them disappear.

Every time we want help from an AI today, we perform the same little ritual: reach for a phone, unlock it, find the right app, type something, attach something, wait, read, and then put the phone away.

It works. But it is still a conversation with a screen.

I kept thinking about the opposite. What if the computer already knew where I was looking? What if I could just ask a question while walking, working, travelling, building something, or standing in front of something I didn't understand?

That question became ASPIRE.

Not a phone on your face. A new interface for intelligence.

The idea was simple enough to explain in one sentence and difficult enough to keep me busy for months: build glasses that can hear me, see what I see, understand what I mean, and respond without asking me to reach for a screen.

There was no single “smart glasses” platform waiting to be assembled. I had to work from the bottom up — camera, microphone, audio, wake detection, connectivity, AI orchestration, tool execution and the interaction model itself.

02 / THE EXPERIENCE

It should feel like talking to someone — not operating a machine.

Just talk.

You shouldn't have to learn a command language to use your own assistant. ASPIRE is designed around natural conversation: ask something, describe something, or tell VIPER what you want to happen.

The system listens for speech instead of keeping a constant stream of audio running, helping reduce unnecessary processing, network traffic and power consumption.

Wake → listen → respondA deliberate interaction loop instead of an always-on cloud microphone.
Voice-first outputResponses are designed to be heard, not read.

Your point of view becomes context.

The camera changes the relationship between you and the AI. Instead of describing a thing and hoping the model understands, you can let it look at the thing with you.

Read a sign. Understand a screen. Ask about an object. Translate something in front of you. The image is not an attachment — it is part of the conversation.

First-person visionThe camera sees from the same perspective as the wearer.
Visual translationAsk VIPER to read or translate visible text.
Focused answersThe system is instructed to focus on the subject that actually matters.

The interesting part isn't seeing. It's knowing why you're showing it.

A camera can capture a picture. An assistant needs to understand the question around that picture.

VIPER combines your spoken intent with the visual context so the same camera can support completely different interactions. “What is this?” is different from “Translate this,” which is different from “Does this code look right?”

Multimodal interactionVoice and vision work together instead of living as separate features.
Context-aware reasoningThe model is guided to answer the user's actual question rather than narrating everything in frame.

And sometimes, the right answer isn't an answer.

VIPER can turn an intention into an action. Ask to take a picture and the system can capture it. Ask about information in connected services and the assistant can use the appropriate tool rather than pretending it already knows.

That is the step from chatbot to assistant: understanding what should happen next.

Take a photoTrigger the camera from a natural voice request.

04 / THE INTELLIGENCE

ASPIRE is the body. VIPER is the mind.

ASPIRE is the physical system: the frame, camera, microphone, audio hardware and embedded controller that sit with you in the real world.

VIPER is the intelligence layer. Its job isn't simply to generate text. It decides what kind of response the user needs, whether visual context matters, whether an external tool should be called, and when an action should happen instead of another sentence being spoken.

That distinction became important as the project grew. A wearable computer has very different constraints from a laptop. Memory is limited. Power matters. Network calls add latency. Audio needs to feel conversational. And the user should never need to think about any of that.

All the complexity should disappear behind one very simple interface: talk to it.

05 / FOR THE CURIOUS

The engineering underneath.

If the first half is the story of what ASPIRE feels like, this is the part for the people who want to know how I actually made it work.

A tiny computer with very non-tiny ambitions.

The current prototype uses a Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense as the embedded core. It provides the camera and microphone interface while keeping the physical system compact enough to experiment with as a wearable.

The challenge is not simply getting each component to work. It is making them work together without turning the glasses into a hot, slow development board strapped to your face.

CONTROLLER

Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense with embedded PSRAM.

CAMERA

OV2640 camera used for first-person visual input.

AUDIO

Digital microphone input with an I2S audio path and dedicated amplifier for output.

Listening only when listening matters.

Continuous audio streaming is expensive. The firmware therefore uses local voice-activity detection based on RMS energy to identify when speech is actually happening.

The current implementation processes audio in blocks, uses a loudness threshold and trailing silence to determine the end of a turn, and places a hard cap on recording time. That turns “always listening” into a much more controlled interaction loop.

LOCAL VAD

RMS-based speech detection runs before network transmission.

TURN CONTROL

Speech debounce and trailing silence prevent accidental short triggers.

FAIL-SAFE

A maximum recording duration prevents uncontrolled buffer growth.

The camera is not an accessory. It is a sensor for context.

The OV2640 frame is captured into available memory and passed through the vision pipeline. Because the physical camera placement introduces an orientation challenge, the system explicitly accounts for the sensor's mounted orientation when interpreting images.

Resolution and image handling are deliberately constrained around the latency and memory budget of the wearable rather than simply maximizing image quality.

CAPTURE

Camera frames are handled directly by the embedded system.

ORIENTATION

Sensor configuration and model-level instructions compensate for the physical mounting geometry.

VISION RULES

VIPER is instructed to avoid inventing visual details and to focus on the requested subject.

From “answer me” to “do this.”

The assistant is given a set of executable tools alongside the user's request. If the model determines that a tool is appropriate, it returns a structured action which the embedded system can execute.

This is what lets the same voice interface move between conversation and action: retrieving information, interacting with connected services, or triggering a camera operation.

TOOL CALLING

Structured model outputs map user intent to executable functions.

CONNECTED SERVICES

Prototype integrations include email, calendar and cloud-storage retrieval.

LOCAL ACTIONS

Hardware operations such as photo capture can be short-circuited locally.

The hard part was deciding what the glasses should not do.

An ESP32-S3 is powerful for its size, but it is not a miniature data centre. Instead of forcing every AI workload onto the frame, ASPIRE treats the glasses as a fast sensory endpoint and distributes heavier reasoning to external compute.

This separation keeps the wearable focused on what it is uniquely good at: sensing the world, capturing the user's intent and responding quickly. The architecture can then evolve independently as the AI and orchestration layer become more capable.

EDGE

Wake detection, audio capture, camera control and hardware actions.

REASONING

Large-model inference handles language, multimodal understanding and tool selection.

ORCHESTRATION

A separation between sensing and intelligence leaves room for a more modular MCP-based architecture.

06 / WHAT'S NEXT

This is a prototype. That's the point.

ASPIRE is not being presented as a finished consumer product. It is a working exploration of what happens when a multimodal AI is given a body, a point of view and a voice.

The next iterations are about making the system feel less like a collection of impressive demos and more like a coherent product: faster interaction, cleaner hardware, better power management, stronger phone connectivity, more modular tool orchestration and a tighter loop between what the wearer sees and what the assistant understands.

Make the interaction faster.

Reduce the distance between speaking, understanding and hearing the response.

Make the architecture modular.

Move toward a cleaner orchestration layer and MCP-style tool connectivity so new capabilities do not require rebuilding the entire wearable.

Make the hardware disappear.

Shrink the electronics, improve the audio experience and keep the system comfortable enough that the technology stops being the thing you notice.

Keep building.

The best version of ASPIRE is probably not the one on this page. That's exactly why the project continues.
